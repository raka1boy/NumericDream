Chapter 1

Starting up

NumericDream is a real-time ray marcher for distance-estimated 3D fractals. It renders on the GPU through WebGPU, draws its interface with Nuklear, and assembles its shader at runtime from whichever formula files you have loaded — so changing a formula recompiles the renderer rather than just changing a number.

Run NumericDream.exe from zig-out, or build and launch in one step with zig build run. Two things must sit next to the executable:

  • wgpu_native.dll — the graphics backend. Without it the app will not start.
  • formulas/ — the shipped formula library. Without it the app still runs, but the in-app Library… browser reports the folder missing and you have to type or browse formula paths by hand.

The window opens at 1280×720 with one fractal (the built-in Mandelbulb), one global light, no fog, and the camera 3.2 units out looking back at the origin. Everything else you add yourself.

Optional: ffmpeg

Animation rendering pipes finished frames straight into ffmpeg to produce anim.mp4. ffmpeg is never bundled — if it is not on your PATH, animation rendering silently falls back to writing a numbered PNG sequence instead and tells you muxing was skipped. Nothing else in the app needs it.

First launch is slow

The very first render after any formula change compiles a large shader from scratch, which can take several seconds. Your GPU driver caches the result on disk, so the same combination is near-instant afterwards. This is normal; see Chapter 23 if a compile never finishes.

Chapter 2

Flying the camera

The camera is a free-fly camera with no up-axis constraint: it has a position and an orientation, and you can roll and loop it as far as you like without ever hitting gimbal lock. Camera input is ignored while the mouse is over a panel, so you can drag a slider without the view lurching.

InputAction
W / SMove forward / backward along the view direction
A / DStrafe left / right
Arrow keysTurn — left/right yaw, up/down pitch, unlimited in both
Q / ERoll right / left around the view axis
Z / CHalve / multiply move speed by 1.5, once per press
F / RZoom in / out — 2D mode only (Chapter 16); no effect otherwise
Mouse dragLook around (same as the arrow keys)
Scroll wheelDolly forward / back, 0.3 units per notch
EscCancel a render in progress

Base move speed is 2.5 units per second before the Z/C multiplier. Since fractal detail is self-similar, expect to lean on Z heavily as you close in — at a hundredth of a unit from the surface, the default speed crosses the entire visible structure in a single frame.

Reset camera, at the bottom of the Fractals panel, returns to the opening view and resets the speed multiplier and the 2D zoom. It deliberately leaves the 2D mode checkbox alone — untick that yourself when you want to go back to ray marching.

Note

The help text at the bottom of the Fractals panel describes WASD as panning left/right/up/down. The actual behaviour is the one in the table above: W/S dolly along the view axis, A/D strafe.

Gizmos

Opening an object's editor window also marks it as selected, and selected objects draw a line gizmo in the viewport:

  • Fractal — an XYZ triad at its offset, rotated by its rotation and stretched by its per-axis scale, so you can read both orientation and axis ratios directly. Red is X, green Y, blue Z.
  • Point light and fog emitter — a world-aligned triad marking the position.
  • Global light — a single line from the world origin pointing toward the light, tinted with the light's own color.
  • Ray light — the position triad, plus a beam line leaving it along the aim direction, fading out from the light's own color.
  • Warp — an oriented triad at its centre, scaled by the size of its region, so the axes show where the deformation's own frame points (Chapter 14). A warp's rotation is load-bearing, which is why it gets an oriented triad rather than a world-aligned one.

Gizmos draw in 2D mode too, projected orthographically onto the cutting plane instead of in perspective (Chapter 16).

Chapter 3

How the interface works

There is one permanent panel — Fractals, docked at the top right — and one permanent strip, the Timeline, along the bottom. Everything else is a floating window you open from a list and close with its × button. All windows are movable and resizable.

Lists and editors

Fractals, lights, fog emitters, and mixins all appear as list rows with a name and a Remove button. Clicking the name toggles that object's editor window open or closed; the object is also gizmo-highlighted in the viewport while its editor is open. You cannot remove the last fractal — a scene always has at least one.

Sliders have adjustable ranges

Every slider in the app carries its own minimum and maximum, and right-clicking a slider opens a small popup with Value, Min and Max fields you can type into. This matters more than it sounds: several defaults are deliberately conservative, and a few (notably the MC Render sample counts) ship with a value above the slider's own maximum, so they get clamped down the moment the panel draws. If a parameter refuses to go where you need it, widen its range rather than assuming that is the limit.

Ranges are saved with the scene, so a widened slider stays widened in that .dream file.

Dragging is continuous; counts are whole

Dragging a slider moves its value by however far the pointer moved, not in fixed increments — every value in the range is reachable, and the exact number sits right-aligned beside the slider's label so you can see what you landed on. Where that is still not precise enough (the epsilon sliders in Chapter 5 live in the fourth and fifth decimal), type the number into the Value field of the right-click popup; it is a plain text box and keeps every digit you give it.

The exception is any slider whose value is a count — march steps, iterations, bounces, samples, Inner max steps. Those step one whole unit at a time and round what they are given, including the ends of their range, since the shader is going to truncate them to integers anyway. A formula's own parameters can opt into this too; see Chapter 20.

Two independent quality sets

The marching quality you see in the viewport and the marching quality used for a final render are separate settings that never influence each other. The Fractals panel tunes the live preview; the Render settings… popup tunes the one-shot export. Getting a clean preview does not automatically get you a clean export, and vice versa. See chapters 4, 5, and 17.

Compiling blocks everything

While a formula is compiling, the window stays responsive but stops drawing new frames, and the Compile button is replaced by a "Compiling…" label. Adding or removing a fractal or a mixin also recompiles, and those two block the app outright until they finish. This is a hard constraint of talking to the GPU driver safely, not a queue you can jump.

Chapter 4

The Fractals panel

The top of the panel controls how the live preview is marched. These settings apply only to the viewport.

Simple renderdefault off

A diagnostic view of the scene. Ticking it opens the Simple render window (the Simple render parts... button brings the window back if you close it) and the viewport follows whatever the window says. It never touches an export, an animation render or the scene file — untick it and the full image is back.

Geometry only (fast), the window's first switch and its default, is the shape and nothing else: flat grey under one lamp mounted on the camera, with no lights, shadows, fog, reflections, refraction or photon gather anywhere in it. It runs from a separate, much smaller shader, so it is far faster than the full render with everything turned off would be — use it to frame a camera, check a silhouette, or hunt for the parameter value where a shape stops being a blob. The photon map is not traced while it is on.

Untick Geometry only and the window becomes a list of the full render's parts, each with its own switch: Colour strips, Specular highlights, Subsurface scattering and Iridescence under Surface; Direct lights, Shadows, Ambient occlusion, Photon map and Sky under Light; Fog, Reflections, Refraction, Dispersion and MC indirect light under Transport; Depth of field under Camera. With everything ticked the image is exactly the normal one; each switch you clear removes that one contribution and nothing else, and a note under the switch says what is left in its place. All on and All off reset the list. This is for finding things: whether a dark patch is shadow or occlusion, what the fog alone is adding, whether the photon map is responsible for a speckle.

Two things to know. Switching a part off is a branch in the same shader, not a different shader, so it saves the work of that part but cannot match Geometry only's speed. And the switches are a view of the scene, not a change to it — the photon map is still traced with glass in the scene even while Refraction is off, so caustics can remain after the glass that threw them has gone opaque. MC Render accumulates as usual under the part switches and restarts whenever one changes.

Max march stepsdefault 128range 32–512

How many steps a view ray may take before it gives up and returns "no hit". Too few and deep or grazing parts of the fractal dissolve into background; too many and every pixel gets slower whether it needed the steps or not. Also caps the cheaper rays derived from it: shadow rays use at most 40 steps, and subsurface scattering's depth probe at most 64. Refraction's interior ray is the exception — it has its own per-material Inner max steps budget and is not bounded by this.

Max distancedefault 24.0range 4–64

How far a ray travels before it is declared a miss, in world units. Anything beyond this is sky. Lower it when you are working close in and want the frame rate back; raise it when distant structure is being cut off.

Preview quality (render scale)default 1.0range 0.1–1.0

Renders the viewport at a fraction of the window's pixel size and upscales the result. 0.5 quarters the pixel count. This is the fastest way to keep a heavy scene interactive while you tune it, and it costs nothing in the final render, which never uses this value.

Reflection bouncesdefault 1range 0–8

How many times a ray may bounce off reflective material before the remaining reflected energy is treated as sky. These are real marched reflections, so a fractal reflects its own geometry. Cost scales with the number of surfaces actually reflective enough to keep bouncing — on a matte scene, raising this is nearly free. Hard-capped at 8 in the shader even if you widen the slider.

Photon mapdefault on

The mirror image of the setting above, run from the lights instead of the camera. Its own group of sliders lives just below, and the whole thing is described in Lights & shadows: Scatter bounces, Photon paths, Gather radius, Photon brightness, Grid size, Retrace delay and Retrace per MC Render sample.

None of these have a separate copy in Render Settings: exported stills and animations use the values set here, so a render always matches the lighting you composed in the viewport.

Chapter 5

Ray march precision

This group decides how close a ray must get to a surface before it counts as a hit, and how much effort the marcher spends double-checking a step it does not trust. The same five sliders appear twice — once in the Fractals panel for the live preview, once in Render settings for the export — with independent values.

The hit tolerance is epsilon_coefficient × distance + epsilon_floor: it grows with distance from the camera, so a surface a long way off is not asked to resolve to the same absolute precision as one right in front of you. That same radius is reused for surface-normal sampling, which is why loosening it also smears shading detail.

Hit epsilon coefficient (× distance)default 0.0005range 0.0001–0.005

The distance-proportional part of the hit tolerance. Lowering it sharpens far-away detail and costs steps; raising it is the quickest way to trade crispness for speed across the whole depth range.

Hit epsilon floordefault 0.00005range 0.000005–0.001

The minimum tolerance, applying right at the camera. Lower this when you are deep-zoomed and near-field detail looks melted; it is the knob that governs how far you can zoom before the surface stops resolving.

HQ footprint budget (px)default 2.0range 0.25–8export only

A last-resort cap, in screen pixels, on how far the export marcher will commit to a step it could not verify as safe. Lower values close the gap that lets thin features slip between samples, at the cost of more steps. Has no effect on the live preview, which never runs the verification path this backs up.

Bisection refine steps (interactive)default 4range 1–12

How many extra probes the preview marcher spends checking a suspect step before committing. Distance estimates for folded and mixed formulas can overshoot; these probes catch the overshoot. Raise it if the preview shows speckled or torn surfaces, lower it for speed.

Bisection refine steps (high quality)default 20range 4–40export only

The same budget for the export path, where the goal is to bisect until the interval is provably below the hit tolerance rather than stop at a fixed count. 20 halvings takes any starting interval below float precision, so the default already means "converge properly".

Fix speckling here first

Torn, noisy, or dotted surfaces are almost always an overshooting distance estimate, not a precision problem. Try the fractal's own Step-size safety factor (Chapter 6) before spending steps on epsilon and bisection.

Empty-space accelerator

Every step of a ray march costs one full evaluation of your formula — and on a ray that misses the fractal entirely, or that crosses a long stretch of open space before reaching it, almost all of those evaluations only ever conclude "still nothing here". The accelerator answers that question far more cheaply, from a precomputed grid, and lets the marcher jump ahead.

The grid is a set of nested cubes centred on the camera, each one twice as wide and twice as coarse as the last. Every cell records a distance that is guaranteed safe to travel from anywhere inside it. Because cell size grows with distance from the camera, so does the size of the jumps — matching the way a pixel's footprint grows, so the grid stays about as detailed as the picture needs at every depth.

It only ever skips space it can prove is empty. Near a surface the cells are far too coarse to help, and there it simply steps aside and lets the exact marcher work as it always has. The image is the same either way — this is purely a speed feature, and turning it off is a fair A/B test of whether it is earning its place on your scene.

The grid is rebuilt automatically, a moment after you stop dragging a slider or flying the camera. While you are still moving it is left alone, so a rebuild never lands in the middle of an interaction. While a fractal edit is unbuilt the accelerator switches itself off rather than trusting a grid that describes the shape you just changed away from — so a slider drag renders at the old, unaccelerated speed, and snaps back a moment after you let go. Exports and animation frames build their own grid, once per image, where the cost is spread over the whole render.

Enableddefault on

Off marches every step exactly. Worth reaching for if a custom formula's distance estimate is unreliable enough to defeat the safety margin below, and as the honest way to measure what the accelerator is actually buying you.

Grid resolution (per cascade)default 64range 16–160

Cells along one edge of one cube. Memory and rebuild time both grow with the cube of this, so 128 costs eight times what 64 does; in return the grid resolves smaller pockets of empty space, at every depth at once. Raise it on scenes with lots of fine open structure to thread through, leave it alone on a solid blob against empty sky where the coarse grid already skips everything worth skipping.

Cascadesdefault 6range 1–8

How many nested cubes. Each one doubles the total reach for a fixed cell size, at a linear cost. The outermost is always sized to Max distance, so adding cascades makes the innermost ones finer rather than extending the range.

Skip safety margindefault 0.92range 0.5–1

Shrinks every recorded jump by this factor. At 1.0 the accelerator trusts the distance estimate exactly; lower values hold some back, which is the knob to reach for if a formula that already needs a low Step-size safety factor shows holes with the accelerator on that it does not show with it off.

Rebuild delay (ms)default 180range 0–1000

How long everything has to sit still before the grid is refilled. Longer keeps rebuilds further out of the way of continuous edits; shorter gets the acceleration back sooner after each one.

Where it does and does not help

The win is on rays that spend their budget crossing emptiness — background pixels, distant cameras, and the shadow rays that have to prove nothing is in the way. A camera buried inside dense structure, where every ray hits something within a few steps, has little empty space to skip and will see little difference.

Chapter 6

Shaping a fractal

Each fractal instance has a placement in the world, a formula, optional mixins, and a color strip. This chapter covers placement; the rest follow.

The transform is applied in the order scale, then rotation, then offset. A fractal is grown or squashed along its own local axes, spun around its own centre, and then moved into place.

Offset X / Y / Zdefault 0range −4…4

Where the fractal's own origin sits in world space. Also the point its rotation and scale pivot around, and where its gizmo triad is drawn.

Scaledefault 1.0range 0.1–4.0

A uniform multiplier on top of the three per-axis values. Use this to resize the whole object without disturbing an axis ratio you have already tuned.

Scale X / Y / Zdefault 1.0range 0.1–4.0

Per-axis stretch along the fractal's own local axes. Non-uniform scale has no single correct distance correction, so the marcher uses the smallest axis — an underestimate, which is always safe but can make steps smaller than necessary along a heavily stretched axis. If a strongly non-uniform shape marches slowly, that is why.

Rotation X / Y / Zdefault 0°range −180…180°

Euler angles in degrees, composed X then Y then Z. The gizmo's three axis lines show exactly where the local axes ended up, so you can read a composed rotation off the viewport instead of decomposing it in your head.

Step-size safety factordefault 1.0range 0.05–1.0

Multiplies every step the marcher takes through this fractal. 1.0 trusts the formula's distance estimate completely; lower values take shorter, more cautious steps. This is the primary cure for speckled, torn, or noise-flecked surfaces — especially on mixed shapes and heavily folded formulas, whose distance estimates are not reliable lower bounds. Turn it down until the surface cleans up, then stop: every reduction costs proportional render time.

Chapter 7

Combining fractals

A scene can hold up to four fractals. The first one seeds the scene; every one after it merges into the accumulated result using its own combine mode, in list order. These modes are the Mandelbulb3D "DE Combinate" family.

Combine mode and blend smoothness only appear on Fractal 2 and later — the first fractal has nothing to combine with.

ModeEffect
UnionThe two shapes simply coexist, with sharp seams where they intersect. Ignores blend smoothness.
Smooth MinSmooth union: the seam is rounded over the blend radius. The default, and the one to reach for first.
Smooth MaxSmooth intersection: only where both shapes exist survives.
Smooth Inv-MaxSmooth subtraction: this fractal is carved out of everything merged before it.
Smooth Min-LinLike Smooth Min, but the blend falls off linearly — a sharper crease at the seam.
Smooth Min-NLinA third falloff shape: flatter at the seam than either of the above.
Smooth MixA plain cross-fade between the two fields, without Smooth Min's rounding bulge.
Blend smoothnessdefault 0.5range 0–2

The blend radius, in world units, over which the two distance fields merge. At 0 every smooth mode collapses to its hard equivalent. Materials blend across exactly the same region as the geometry, so a wide blend also cross-fades the two fractals' colors instead of snapping between them at the seam.

Combining vs. mixing

Combine modes merge separate, independently placed shapes. Mixins (Chapter 9) do something different: they fuse a second formula into a single shape at the iteration level, producing geometry neither formula makes alone.

Chapter 8

Formulas & the library

Every fractal instance and every mixin has a formula slot. Empty means the built-in Mandelbulb; otherwise it holds a .wgsl file loaded from disk. The controls are identical in both places.

Path field + Browse…

The file to load. Type a path or pick one with the native file dialog. Setting the path does not load anything on its own.

Library…

Opens the Formula Library browser onto this slot. Clicking any formula there fills in the path and compiles it immediately.

Compile

Reads the file, checks it defines the three required functions, parses its // @param declarations into sliders, and rebuilds the shader with it in place. On failure the previous formula stays loaded and the error appears in red underneath.

Parametersup to 8

One slider per @param the file declares, in declaration order, with the name, range, and default the file itself specifies. These are the formula's own knobs — Power and Iterations for the Mandelbulb, Scale/MinRadius/FixedRadius/FoldingLimit/Iterations for the Amazing Box, and so on. Changing them is instant: they are uniforms, not code, so no recompile is involved.

The Formula Library browser

The library lists every .wgsl file in the formulas/ folder next to the executable, non-recursively, up to 128 files. Each row shows a name and its labels.

Both come from the filename, using the convention name_labelA_labelB.wgsl: the first underscore-separated token is the display name, every token after it is a filter label. So mandelboxtwist_box_boxfold_twist.wgsl shows as mandelboxtwist carrying the labels box, boxfold, and twist.

The chips above the list toggle those labels as filters, and the filter is an AND: selecting box and twist shows only formulas carrying both. Clear filters resets them. Rescan re-reads the folder, which you need after adding or renaming files while the app is running — the scan otherwise happens only once, the first time you open the browser.

Naming your own formulas to this convention is all it takes to have them appear with working filter labels.

Chapter 9

Mixins

A mixin fuses another formula into this fractal's shape rather than adding a second shape to the scene. It is the equivalent of Mandelbulb3D's "Alternate" hybrid mode.

Normally a fractal iterates its own formula a set number of times and then converts the result into a distance. With mixins active, a single shared trajectory is built by cycling: the base formula runs for a few iterations, then each mixin in turn runs for its own count, then back to the base, repeating until the total iteration budget is spent. Only the base formula converts the result into a distance, which is why a mixed shape keeps the base's overall character while the mixins etch their folds into its surface. A Mandelbulb base with a Menger mixin reads as a Mandelbulb whose surface follows a Menger lattice.

Add up to three mixins per fractal with Add mixin. Each appears as a list row; clicking it opens an editor holding just that mixin's formula section, with its own file and its own parameter sliders. Adding or removing a mixin recompiles the shader and blocks until done.

Base iterations / cycledefault 2range 0–8

How many consecutive iterations of the base formula run at the start of each cycle. Whichever formula dominates the front of the cycle sets the silhouette, so this is the main control over which formula "wins" the overall shape.

Mixin N iterations / cycledefault 1range 0–8

How many iterations that mixin contributes per cycle. 0 removes it from the cycle without deleting it.

Total mixed iterationsdefault 12range 1–48

How many iterations run in total across the whole cycling sequence. This is the depth control for a mixed shape — the equivalent of an ordinary formula's Iterations parameter, and by far the most expensive slider here.

While a mix is active

Each formula's own Iterations parameter is ignored — the three sliders above govern the whole cycle. Mixins also contribute geometry only: coloring always comes from the parent fractal's own color strip.

Mixed shapes have less reliable distance estimates than single formulas, because folds from formulas with different conventions are being composed. If a mix speckles, lower the instance's Step-size safety factor.

Chapter 10

Color strips & materials

Each fractal is colored by an orbit trap: while the formula iterates, the renderer tracks how close the trajectory came to the origin, and uses that value to sample a gradient. Points that dive close to the origin land at one end of the strip; points that stay out land at the other. This is what paints structural bands rather than a flat coat of paint.

Editing the strip

  • Left-click empty strip space — add a stop there, up to 16.
  • Press a marker — select it; its color picker and material sliders appear below.
  • Drag a marker left or right — slide that color along the strip.
  • Right-click a marker — delete it.

Stops are drawn and sampled in position order regardless of the order you created them, and the gradient interpolates linearly between neighbours. Before the first stop and after the last, the color is held flat. Material properties interpolate right along with the color, so a stop is a full material, not just a hue.

Dragging tunes the relationships

Where a stop sits decides both how wide its own band is and how abrupt the blend into its neighbours is: two markers close together give a sharp edge between those colors, two far apart give a long ramp. Dragging is how that is tuned by eye — the strip and the render both update live as the marker moves. A marker can be dragged straight past its neighbours; the strip re-sorts itself, and the stop stays selected throughout, so the sliders below keep editing the color you are holding. Dragging past either end clamps at 0 or 1.

Color

The stop's RGB color, edited in the picker that drops down from the swatch.

Glossinessdefault 0.3range 0–1

Specular tightness and strength together: 0 is a broad, weak highlight, 1 a small hard one. Affects only the highlight, not reflections.

Transparencydefault 0range 0–1

How much of what lies behind the surface shows through. Above 0, the renderer bends the ray into the shape, marches through the interior looking for where it re-emerges, bends it again on the way out, and shades whatever it finds. Fractal interiors are full of cavities, so this is an approximation — and a ray that never finds its way out within its Inner max steps budget is treated as absorbed, leaving the surface opaque for that pixel. This is the master switch for the whole refraction group below: at 0, IOR, Abbe number, Roughness and Inner max steps all do nothing — with one exception: IOR is also the substrate index under an iridescent film (see Iridescence below), so it still matters there on an opaque material.

Reflectivenessdefault 0.1range 0–1

How much of the surface's response comes from a real marched reflection instead of its own shading. Reflection rays bounce off whatever they actually hit, including the same fractal, up to the Reflection bounces limit. Reflected surfaces are shaded without shadows — the saving is large and the difference is small.

IORdefault 1.5range 1–3

Index of refraction for the transmitted ray, quoted the way glass catalogues quote it — at the yellow helium d-line, 587.6 nm. 1.0 sends the ray straight through unbent, 1.33 is water, 1.5 window glass, 1.77 sapphire, 2.42 diamond.

IOR also sets the critical angle, and on a faceted shape that matters more than the bending does. A ray that entered from air can only ever meet the inside of a wall at up to the critical angle (41.8° at 1.5, 24.4° at 2.42), so any face roughly perpendicular to the one it came in through will total-internally-reflect it — the wall behaves as a mirror and the light stays inside. The renderer follows up to four such bounces before giving up and standing in the sky, which is what makes a Menger's cross-cut holes read as glass rather than as flat panels. Raising IOR makes those bounces more frequent, so a high setting looks busier and costs more.

Abbe numberdefault 0 (off)range 0–120

Dispersion — the reason a prism throws a rainbow. Real materials have a slightly different IOR at every wavelength, so red and blue bend by different amounts and separate into colored fringes along refracted edges. The Abbe number is the optical industry's measure of how strongly a material does this, and it runs backwards from every other slider in this panel: lower disperses more. Roughly 20 is dense flint glass (showy, obvious rainbows), 30–40 is a heavy crystal, 64 is ordinary BK7 crown glass, and 90+ is barely perceptible. 0 is a special value meaning off, and is the default.

The spread is a real one. Light in this renderer is carried as a spectrum — eight wavelengths, drawn fresh across the whole visible band for every path and turned back into color through the CIE color matching functions — rather than as three fixed red, green and blue taps. That is what puts the yellows, oranges and cyans in a rainbow: a scheme built on the three sRGB primaries cannot produce them at all, and renders the overlap of its red and blue fringes as magenta, a color no prism has ever thrown.

It is off by default because it is not free in the preview. Following all eight wavelengths through a prism means eight separately refracted paths, and a refracted path is the most expensive thing this renderer does — so at a dispersing surface each sample commits to one randomly chosen wavelength at eightfold weight. Averaged over enough samples that is exactly the full spectral answer at an eighth of the cost, but a single frame of it is noisy: expect colored grain on transparent surfaces in the live view. Turn on MC Render and it converges to clean spectral edges along with everything else, and image and animation exports get it for free. Nothing that isn't dispersing pays for any of this — a neutral surface reconstructs exactly, with no added noise at all.

Roughnessdefault 0range 0–1

Frosted glass. Scatters the normal the transmitted ray refracts against, independently where it enters and where it leaves — both faces etched, which is what a sandblasted surface actually is. At 0 the normal is untouched and the material is optically clear; low values blur what shows through while keeping its shape recognisable; high values diffuse it into a glow. The slider is squared internally, so most of its useful travel sits in the bottom third where barely-frosted lives.

Like dispersion this is one random sample per frame, so it grains in the live view and converges under MC Render.

Inner max stepsdefault 64range 8–256

How many marching steps the transmitted ray gets to find its way back out of the solid. A ray that spends its budget without re-emerging is treated as absorbed and the surface stays opaque for that pixel, so if a thick or convoluted transparent shape is refusing to go see-through in places — solid patches, seams where two instances meet — this is the slider to raise.

Steps are not uniform: they start at the marcher's own hit tolerance and grow as the ray penetrates, so a bigger budget buys finer sampling of the interior rather than simply more reach. Total reach stays around nine world units at any budget, bounded by Max distance.

It multiplies the cost of every transparent pixel directly, so it is the most expensive knob in the group. Hard-capped at 512 internally regardless of how far you widen the slider's range by right-clicking it.

Why interior steps have to grow

A distance estimator is only obliged to be correct outside the shape, and most are not meaningful inside one. Menger3's, for instance, is the box distance with its interior term left off, so it returns exactly zero at every point inside a solid region rather than a negative depth. The interior march therefore gets no guidance at all in there and has to walk on its floor step alone. When that floor was a fixed 0.002, a 64-step budget could only ever penetrate 0.128 world units — thinner than the wall of any low-iteration shape — so simple fractals could never be seen through at any setting, while intricate ones, whose shells are thinner than that, worked fine. Growing the floor with depth is what makes the budget mean something.

Subsurface scatteringdefault 0range 0–1

How much light travels through the material and comes back out toward the camera — the wax, jade, marble and skin family of looks, as opposed to Transparency's see-through glass. Above 0 the renderer probes how deep the solid runs behind the hit and applies Beer–Lambert absorption over that depth, so thin spines, fins and edges glow while thick masses stay solid. The transmitted light is tinted by the stop's own color on the way out.

The slider is strength and reach at once: low values absorb quickly, lighting only a thin rim; high values let light carry much deeper for a soft, lit-from-within body. It also softens the terminator, letting diffuse light bleed a little past the point where the surface turns away from a light.

Iridescence

A thin transparent coating over the surface — the soap-bubble, oil-on-water, beetle-shell family of color. Light bouncing off the top of the film and light bouncing off the bottom of it have travelled paths that differ by twice the film's thickness, so at every wavelength the two either reinforce or cancel, and which they do depends on the wavelength: the reflection becomes a fringe pattern across the spectrum, and that pattern slides as the angle of view changes. The renderer evaluates this per wavelength, on the same eight-wavelength spectrum dispersion uses, so the colors are the real ones — a fringe peaking at 550 nm comes out the green it should, not whatever a red-green-blue approximation could reach.

The physical film would only ever show in the specular reflection, which on a matte fractal is a thin rim at grazing angles. Here the fringe color is applied to the whole surface — the diffuse color, the highlight and the marched reflection alike — so it reads on an ordinary opaque fractal the way pearlescent paint does. Bounce light and caustics off an iridescent surface carry the same color. Transmission through a transparent material is not tinted. The film is only evaluated from outside a solid, so the inside faces of a transparent shape ignore it.

Strengthdefault 0 (off)range 0–1

How much of the fringe color is applied. 0 is the master off switch for the whole group and costs nothing; 1 is the full interference pattern. Because the pattern is a real reflectance, its dark wavelengths are genuinely dark — at full strength a strongly-contrasting film darkens the surface overall as well as coloring it, so back off here rather than on Film IOR if you want the hue without the loss of brightness.

Film thickness (nm)default 400range 0–1500

Physical thickness of the coating in nanometres, which sets how many fringes fit across the visible band and therefore which colors appear. Below about 100 the film is thinner than any visible wavelength and barely colors at all. 200–500 gives the broad, saturated two-tone sweeps of a soap bubble; 600–1000 packs several fringes in, for the busier pink-green-pink bands of an oil slick; beyond that the fringes crowd closer than eight wavelengths can resolve and the color washes toward a grainy pastel that MC Render will settle. Animate this to make the colors flow.

Film IORdefault 1.8range 1–3

The coating's own index of refraction — the saturation control, and the reason it is a real number rather than a slider from 0 to 1. The depth of the fringes is set by how strongly light reflects at the film's two faces, which is the contrast between the film's index and the air above it on one side and the stop's IOR on the other. A film whose index matches the stop's IOR has no bottom face, optically, and shows nothing; the further from both 1.0 and the IOR it sits, the deeper the fringes. 1.33 (soap) over the default 1.5 substrate is a delicate pastel; 1.8 is vivid; 2.4 goes near-black at its cancelled wavelengths. Note that the substrate index is the stop's IOR slider even on an opaque material, so it is worth a glance at that value if a film refuses to color.

Angle scaledefault 1range 0–2

Multiplies the angle between the view direction and the surface normal before the fringe is evaluated at it. 1 is physical: color shifts with angle exactly as a real film does. A fractal's normals point everywhere at once, though, so at 1 a detailed surface can break into a confetti of every color the film has, with no readable sweep to it. Lowering this tames that — at 0 the whole surface is evaluated head-on and wears a single fringe color, and values in between keep a gentle shift across the large forms while calming the small ones. Above 1 exaggerates the shift; the angle is held short of grazing internally so this never blows up.

Normal perturbationdefault 0range 0–1

Bends the normal the film sees with a fixed three-dimensional noise, for the swirling, mottled look of oil on water. Only the film's color responds; lighting, reflections and refraction all keep the true normal, so the surface shows swirling color without appearing dented. The noise is fixed in world space rather than drawn afresh per frame, so the swirls stay put as the camera moves, are the same from frame to frame of an animation, and add no grain to the live preview or to MC Render.

Perturbation scaledefault 0.2range 0.01–2

Feature size of that noise, in world units — the width of one swirl. Match it to the scale of the shape you are looking at: a whole-fractal shot wants something near the default, a close-up of one region wants it much smaller. Does nothing while Normal perturbation is 0.

Where subsurface scattering costs you

It is the only material slider that adds a march of its own: one probe into the interior at every visible hit where it is above 0. Left at 0 the whole path is skipped and costs nothing, so a scene that does not use it is unaffected. The back-scatter term deliberately ignores shadows — the surface's own body is exactly what a shadow ray would find in the way, and a lit-from-behind edge is the case the effect exists for. Reflection bounces and MC Render's indirect bounces shade without it, the same way they already shade without shadows.

A fresh fractal starts with three stops: near-black blue at 0, ember orange at 0.5, and pale sand at 1.0, with glossiness and reflectiveness rising along the strip.

Chapter 11

Lights & shadows

A scene holds up to eight lights. There is also a small constant ambient term and the sky itself, which lights the scene as well as backing it (Chapter 12) — so a scene with no lights at all is dim but not black, and a scene with a bright sky and no lights is lit entirely by its environment.

Typedefault Global

Point shines from a position with inverse-square falloff — brightness drops fast, so a point light close to the surface usually needs a much higher brightness than you would expect. Global is a directional sun: it shines uniformly from a direction with no falloff and no position. Ray is a point that only emits into a cone: it has both a position and an aim, keeps the point light's inverse-square falloff, and adds a Spread control that runs from a laser to a flashlight.

Colordefault white

The light's tint, multiplied by brightness.

Brightnessdefault 1.0range 0–4

Overall intensity. Output is tone-mapped at the end of the pipeline, so pushing this high compresses rather than clips.

Position X / Y / Zdefault 2, 3, 2range −10…10point & ray

World position the light shines from.

Direction X / Y / Zdefault 0.6, 0.8, 0.4range −1…1global & ray

For a global light this points toward the light — the direction you would look to see it, not the direction its light travels. For a ray light the sense is reversed: it is the direction the beam actually travels, away from the light's position. Either way it is normalized, so only the ratio matters.

Spread (deg)default 25range 0–60ray only

The beam's divergence — how fast it widens as it travels. A ray light is a real beam rather than a cone tapering to a point: it leaves the emitter already 0.05 units across and grows from there, so its radius at distance t is 0.05 + t · tan(spread). The editor prints that radius at 5 units next to the slider, which is usually more informative than the angle.

At 0 the beam does not widen at all. It is a hairline cylinder that keeps the same width and the same brightness however far it travels — a laser. Turn it up and the beam opens into a flashlight cone; because it is widening in proportion to distance by then, it also picks up the usual inverse-square dimming for free. Most of the useful range is below 45°, which is why the slider stops at 60; right-click it if you want a wider wash.

Spread and Brightness interact strongly, and deliberately: brightness is the beam's power, and narrowing the beam pushes that same power through a smaller cross-section. Going from a 25° flashlight to a laser concentrates it by a factor of several hundred, so a laser at the brightness that suited the cone will be a blown-out white line. Expect to drop Brightness by a lot when you tighten the spread — that concentration is what makes a laser read as a laser.

Cast shadowsdefault on

Whether this light is occluded by geometry. Shadow rays are the single most expensive part of surface shading — each one marches up to 40 steps, per light, per surface. Switching this off on fill lights is one of the biggest speedups available. It also controls whether the light carves shafts through volumetric fog: a light that does not shadow surfaces does not shaft through fog either.

A shadow ray answers one question only: is there a straight, unobstructed line from this surface to that light. Anything that gets to a surface by another route — through glass, off a wall, around a corner — is the photon map's to deliver, and it delivers it in the right place rather than approximately.

Light through glass is traced forward from the emitter and bends at every interface, so it lands where the geometry sends it — letting the shadow ray contribute as well would draw the same light twice. With the photon map switched off in the Fractals panel, shadow rays fall back to dimming and tinting the light that crosses glass instead.

Hard shadowsdefault off

Replaces the penumbra with a crisp edge. Costs the same as soft shadows.

Shadow softnessdefault 8.0range 1–64

Penumbra width, inverted: higher values give crisper shadows, low values give broad, diffuse ones. Only shown when Hard shadows is off.

The photon map

Direct light is easy: a surface asks each light “is there a clear line between us”, and if there is, it is lit. Everything else light does — bending through glass, focusing into a bright caustic on the floor, bouncing off a coloured wall and tinting the room, shafting through fog after it has already been redirected once — cannot be answered from the receiving end at all. You cannot find out where refracted light converges by looking outward from the place it converged.

So that half is simulated from the other end. Before each frame that needs one, the renderer fires several hundred thousand photons out of every light in the scene and follows each one through the geometry, letting every surface it meets reflect it, bend it, trap it by total internal reflection or scatter it, and recording what it carries as it goes. Shading then simply looks up what landed nearby. Caustics, bounce light and refracted light shafts all fall out of that without being special-cased, because they are just what photons do.

Why photons

A curved transparent surface is a lens: every part of a beam’s width meets a differently-angled normal and bends by a different amount, which is what makes light converge to a focus and throw a caustic. Photons reproduce that because each one meets the surface at its own point with its own normal. Photon paths depend only on the lights and the geometry — not on the camera — so they are traced once per scene instead of once per pixel. Flying the camera around retraces nothing.

What the photon map carries, and what it does not

Nothing is recorded until a photon has scattered at least once. The straight run from a light to the first thing it hits is direct lighting, and direct lighting is still analytic — far cleaner, and exactly right. The two never overlap, which is why they can simply be added together.

Photons land in two places. On surfaces, where they become the caustic under a glass sphere, the coloured bounce on the wall beside a lit one, and the light on the inside of a hollow shape. And in fog, where they become the shafts a beam draws after it has been redirected — including the converging cone inside a transparent object, if a fog emitter overlaps it. Photons crossing empty space record nothing, since there is nothing there for them to scatter off.

Every light kind emits: a point light over the whole sphere, a global light off a disk facing the scene, a ray light across its own beam aperture. A ray light’s photons reproduce exactly the beam profile its Spread describes, so nothing about how a laser or a flashlight looks has changed — it is the same beam, made of photons instead of an equation. The sky can emit too, off by default: see Sky photons in Chapter 12.

What happens at each surface is decided by the same interface model the camera uses: reflect, refract straight through into the material, ricochet inside it by total internal reflection, leave through the far side and carry on, or scatter diffusely — chosen from Reflectiveness, Transparency, IOR and Roughness, plus Fresnel, which makes any surface a mirror at a grazing enough angle. A material with a nonzero Abbe number splits its photons by wavelength, so light through a prism throws a spectrum rather than a white smear.

The controls

Scatter bouncesdefault 2range 0–8

How many times a photon may scatter before it is dropped. 0 leaves the map empty — with no scattering there is no indirect light, and direct light was never the photon map’s to carry. 2 is enough for the case this exists for: into the glass, out of the glass, onto the floor.

Photon pathsdefault 262144range 4096–2097152

How many photons each trace fires, shared out evenly across the lights. The biggest quality/time dial here. A caustic’s sharpness is set by the gather radius below; its cleanliness is set by how many photons landed inside that radius, and this is the only thing that raises that number. Worth raising for exports, where the trace happens once for the whole image and its cost is nothing next to the render.

Gather radiusdefault 0.05range 0.002–1.0

Every photon within this distance of a shaded point contributes to it, so this is the resolution limit of every caustic in the image. Smaller resolves finer structure — but it also finds proportionally fewer photons inside itself, so drop it without raising the path count and a caustic turns to speckle. Treat the two as one control with two halves.

Photon brightnessdefault 1.0range 0–8

Scales everything the photon map contributes. 1.0 is the physically consistent value and matches the direct lighting it adds to — the estimator is normalised, so it needs no tuning to be correct. The dial exists because caustics are the one part of the image whose strength depends on a sampling budget, and being able to push them without re-lighting the scene is worth having.

Grid size (power of two)default 16range 12–18

How many cells the spatial hash holds, as a power of two. Costs memory linearly — the status line above the sliders prints the figure — and buys a lower load factor: fewer distant regions sharing a cell, and fewer cells overflowing what they can store. Worth raising on a scene where the light is concentrated into a small region, which is exactly what a caustic is.

Retrace delay (ms)default 180range 0–1000

How long the scene has to sit still before a retrace. Dragging a light around costs one trace when you let go, not one per frame. The existing photons keep being used in the meantime — a slightly stale caustic for a fifth of a second is far less distracting than every caustic in the scene blinking out and back on with each edit.

Retrace per MC Render sampledefault on

Whether each accumulated MC Render sample gets its own freshly seeded set of photons. On, the caustics converge along with the rest of the image, which is the only way to get a clean one. Off, a single trace stands for the whole accumulation — much faster while composing a shot, at the cost of caustics that stay exactly as grainy as one trace makes them.

Exports always trace once for the whole image regardless of this setting, because an export draws every sample of a tile before moving on to the next tile — a per-sample trace there would fire once per tile per sample. Raise Photon paths for an export instead; it buys the same cleanliness, and an export has the time.

What it costs

A trace is a few tens of milliseconds at the default settings — comparable to an accelerator rebuild, and it lands on the same terms: after the scene settles, never while you are dragging. What it costs at draw time is a lookup of the eight cells around each shaded point and each fog step, which is why the gather radius is tied to the grid’s own cell size. The one setting that can genuinely hurt is a large path count with per-sample retracing on, since that pays for the whole trace again on every accumulated sample.

Ambient occlusion

Creases and enclosed pockets darken automatically. The renderer probes outward along the surface normal and compares how far it walked against how much clearance it actually finds, at a radius that scales with camera distance so the effect tracks whatever detail level is currently visible. There is nothing to configure — it is on always, and it is what keeps deep fractal structure legible without lighting every crevice individually.

Chapter 12

Sky & environment

The sky is the backdrop a ray sees when it escapes the fractal, and it is also the light that backdrop casts back onto the geometry. There is deliberately no separate "background colour": both are the same radiance field, sampled in different directions. That is what makes a bright sky actually light the scene instead of sitting behind it like a painted wall.

It is one property of the scene rather than a list of objects, so it opens from a single button in the Fractals panel rather than from a removable row. The button is labelled with the current source — Sky: Procedural or Sky: Image — so the panel says which one you are on without opening anything.

Old scenes look the same

The defaults are a near-black gradient; the presets are where the useful skies live.

Procedural

A three-colour vertical gradient with an optional sun disc. It costs nothing but a handful of uniforms, works in the photon prepass, and is the source the app falls back to whenever an image is unavailable.

Preset5 presets

A dropdown of named starting points: Classic (dim) — the pre-feature look, Daylight, Sunset, Overcast, and Studio. Picking one overwrites the gradient colours, the horizon falloff and the whole sun group, and switches the source back to Procedural. It deliberately leaves Sky brightness, Rotation, Sky photons and the image path alone — those are choices about the scene rather than about which sky it is.

Zenith / Horizon / Ground

The three colours: straight up, at the horizon, and straight down. Above the horizon the gradient runs horizon→zenith; below it, horizon→ground.

Horizon falloffdefault 1.0range 0.2–8

The exponent shaping the upward blend. 1 is a straight linear ramp — the pre-feature look. Higher values pull the horizon colour down into a tighter band just above the horizon, which is the shape a real atmosphere has; Sunset uses 4.5 for exactly that. The underside has no atmospheric profile to imitate, so the blend down to the ground colour stays linear regardless.

Sun discdefault off

Paints a bright disc onto the gradient and reveals the five controls below it. Ignored in Image mode, where the image carries its own sun.

Sun X / Y / Zdefault 0.4, 0.55, 0.3range −1…1

Points toward the sun — the direction you would look to see it, the same sense a global light's direction uses. Normalized, so only the ratio matters; a zero-length direction switches the disc off rather than smearing it over the whole sky.

Sun radius (deg)default 3.0range 0.1–30

Angular radius of the disc. The real sun is about 0.27°, and the default is deliberately more than ten times that: a disc that small is missed by almost every sample a path tracer throws at it, so it reads as noise rather than as sun. Widen it when you want softer, cleaner sun lighting; tighten it only if you can afford the samples. The outer 15% of the radius is softened rather than cut hard, which keeps the disc from stair-stepping and keeps what it lights from banding.

Sun brightnessdefault 20range 0–200

How much brighter than the gradient the disc is. The numbers are large because a sun genuinely is orders of magnitude brighter than the sky around it, and the output is tone-mapped at the end of the pipeline.

Sun colordefault warm white

The disc's tint, multiplied by its brightness.

The disc is not a light

It is part of the environment, so it casts no sharp shadows of its own: what it does is light the scene through the same occlusion-aware — and therefore noisier — paths the rest of the sky does. For crisp shadows from a sun, add a Global light (Chapter 11) aimed the same way. The two are meant to be used together; the disc is what that light looks like when you turn round and face it.

Image

An equirectangular (latitude/longitude) environment map loaded off disk — the 2:1 panorama format every HDRI library ships. It is sampled by longitude and latitude, so the image's horizontal centre faces −Z by default and Rotation spins it from there.

Path field + Browse… / Load / Clear

Browse picks a file and loads it immediately; Load re-reads whatever the path field holds, which is how you pick up an edited file; Clear unloads the image and empties the path. A failed load leaves whatever was loaded before in place.

Status line

Green on success with the decoded size, and HDR when the file carried real above-1.0 radiance; red with the error otherwise. Images larger than 4096 pixels on an edge — or than your device's own texture limit — are halved until they fit, and the line says what they were scaled down to.

Use a .hdr file if you can. An environment map's whole job is to carry radiance well above 1.0, and an 8-bit PNG or JPEG cannot: its brightest pixel is white. Those formats load fine (PNG, JPEG, TGA and BMP all do) and make perfectly good backdrops, but they light a scene flatly, because there is no sun in them that is any brighter than the clouds.

Image mode with nothing loaded is not an error state — it renders the procedural gradient instead, and the panel says so. What it will never do is show you a flat grey wall.

Shared controls

Sky brightnessdefault 1.0range 0–8

Multiplies the whole environment, gradient or image, backdrop and lighting together. 0 is a black sky that casts nothing.

Rotation (deg)default 0range 0–360

Spins the environment about the vertical axis. It is applied to the lookup direction rather than to the image, so it costs the same either way and turns a procedural sun exactly as it turns an HDRI.

Sky photons (bounced sky light)default off

Adds the environment to the photon prepass as an emitter, so sky light that bounces off the fractal — or refracts through it — is carried the same way a light's is (Chapter 11). The sky always lights the scene directly; this is only about the indirect half.

It takes a share of the existing Photon paths budget rather than adding to it, and a large fraction of sky photons cross the scene without touching anything, so on a scene lit mainly by its own lights it makes the caustics noisier in exchange for very little. It earns its cost on a scene whose sky is the light. With the photon map switched off it does nothing at all, and the panel says as much.

How the sky lights things

Three paths read the same environment, which is why they cannot disagree:

  • A camera ray that escapes the fractal returns the sky directly — that is the backdrop.
  • With MC Render on, the traced indirect bounce returns the sky when it escapes. While the photon map is also on, that bounce is reduced to a sky-visibility probe, since the map already carries everything else.
  • With Sky photons on, the prepass emits from the sky as well, adding the bounced and refracted share.

2D mode renders a flat cross section with no rays and no lighting (Chapter 16), so the sky has nothing to do there — it is simply absent, and comes back when you switch the mode off.

Sky settings are saved with the scene, and the image is saved as a path, exactly as a formula is: the .dream file stays small text, and the pixels are decoded off disk again when the scene opens. A path that no longer resolves reports the failure in the status line and falls back to the gradient rather than refusing to open the scene. The sky is not keyframed — it holds still for a whole animation.

Chapter 13

Volumetric fog

A fog emitter is a soft-edged sphere of participating medium, placed in the scene the same way a light or a fractal is. Up to four can be active; where they overlap their densities add while their colors and scattering blend by contribution.

Fog is what turns lights into visible shafts. Any light with Cast shadows on is tested for visibility at each fog sample, so beams carve around whatever the fractal blocks — moving a light or the emitter reshapes the shafts live.

It is also what makes a ray light's beam visible in mid-air rather than only where it lands. A narrow-spread ray light crossing an emitter draws a bright line through the haze; widen the spread and the same light becomes a cone of lit fog.

Why a laser stays clean in fog

The fog march samples the volume at a fixed number of steps spread over the whole ray, so a step is routinely much wider than a laser is thick. Sampling the beam at one point per step would therefore miss it on most steps and render it as sparse grain that fades out entirely as the spread approaches 0. Ray lights are instead integrated across each step analytically — the beam's Gaussian cross-section has a closed-form integral along a straight segment — so a beam deposits the right amount of light no matter how coarsely the volume is being marched. That is why a laser draws a continuous hairline here and does not need the fog sample count raised to find it.

Occlusion is still tested once per step, at the step's own sample point, so a beam threading a gap narrower than a step can pick up slightly the wrong shadowing. Raising Fog samples in Render Settings tightens that up on a still.

Densitydefault 1.0range 0–8

Thickness at the core. This governs both how much it obscures and how brightly it is lit, since the same coefficient drives absorption and in-scattering. High density plus a shadowing light is the recipe for pronounced god rays; it also hides whatever is behind it.

The most expensive feature in the app

Every fog sample re-marches the scene toward every shadowing light. The live view uses 28 steps per ray with 10-step light checks; an export uses 64 and 24, and then multiplies the whole thing by the Fog samples setting. Budget accordingly, and see Chapter 18 for why animation frames should keep fog samples at 1.

Chapter 14

Warps

A warp is a scene object that deforms space itself inside a bounded region. It does not move the fractal and it does not bend the rays: it changes the question the renderer asks. Instead of evaluating the fractal at the point a ray has reached, it evaluates it at wherever the warp sends that point — so the shape appears twisted, inverted, tiled or rippled while the rays through it stay perfectly straight.

Up to four warps live in their own list in the Fractals panel, below the fog emitters. Each row is labelled with its deformation (Warp 1: Twist), since the number alone would tell you nothing, and clicking it opens that warp's editor.

Warps are global, and they compose

A warp is not attached to a fractal. Every warp in the scene applies to the whole scene, before each fractal's own offset, rotation and scale — so a twist twists all four instances together, as one body of space.

The list composes in order: twist-then-invert is not the same shape as invert-then-twist, and reordering is a real edit rather than a cosmetic one. What does not compose is the region of influence. Each warp's influence is measured at the original world point, not at wherever an earlier warp in the list dragged that point to, so a warp's region stays exactly where you put it however much space upstream of it is being deformed.

The first warp costs a recompile

Adding the first warp to a scene — or removing the last one — rebuilds the shader, the same way loading a formula does. Editing a warp you already have never does: warps are otherwise pure uniform data, so every slider here is instant.

The reason is compile time. Warp evaluation sits inside scene_de, which is inlined at around thirty places, and carrying it unconditionally measured at +5.1 seconds on a cold compile (13.9s → 19.0s on an AMD 780M) — a tax every scene would pay whether or not it had a warp in it. So the evaluators are spliced in only when the scene has one, and a scene with none compiles exactly what it did before warps existed. See Chapter 20.

  • Global — no boundary at all: the warp acts everywhere at a flat strength. Its radius slider becomes a Safety radius, which does not bound where the warp acts; it is only the distance the marcher sizes its safety margin against, which a twist needs because a twist's stretch grows with distance from its axis.
Strengthdefault 1.0range 0–1

Master amount, and the natural thing to keyframe: animating a warp from 0 to 1 grows the deformation in place rather than sliding it through the scene.

Edge falloffdefault 0.5range 0–8sphere & box

Width, in world units, of the band just inside the boundary over which the warp fades in. A soft edge is the forgiving case for the marcher and the default for that reason. It is also what makes Rotate interesting: a rigid rotation that fades in across a band is a shear.

0 is a hard edge, and it is exact rather than merely tolerated. A hard edge makes the distance field genuinely discontinuous — the estimate on the near side describes undeformed geometry and says nothing whatsoever about the deformed geometry waiting on the far side — and no safety factor fixes a discontinuity. So instead of fudging it, the marcher clamps its steps to the boundary: it walks up to the seam in shrinking steps and crosses it by a hair, with an honest distance field on both sides. The cost is extra steps near the seam, nowhere else.

Centre X / Y / Zdefault 0range −10…10

Where the region sits, and the point the deformation is measured from — a twist's axis passes through it, an inversion is centred on it.

Rotation X / Y / Zdefault 0°range −180…180°

Euler angles in degrees, composed X then Y then Z, exactly as a fractal instance's are (Chapter 6). This aims both the region and the deformation itself, so it is load-bearing rather than decorative: it is how you point a twist's axis or a bend's plane anywhere other than along the world axes. A selected warp draws an oriented triad at its centre, scaled by the region's size, so you can read where those axes ended up.

Step safetydefault 1.0range 0.05–2.0

The same escape hatch a fractal's Step-size safety factor is (Chapter 6), for the warp's own distance correction. The correction should already make marching safe, so this is for when its bound turns out pessimistic — steps needlessly small, raise it — or when a formula's distance estimate was already optimistic before the warp got to it — surfaces tearing, lower it.

Why a warp costs steps at all

Sphere tracing needs a lower bound on the distance to the surface, and a warp that stretches space inflates the fractal's own estimate by however much it stretched — so de(W(p)) is not a distance estimate any more, and marching on it would step straight through surfaces. The fix is to divide by a bound on the local stretch, computed on the CPU once per edit so the shader only has to multiply.

That bound has two halves, kept apart because they peak in different places: the deformation's own stretch, which scales with how strongly the warp is acting, and the extra stretch the falloff blend introduces, which is zero both fully inside and fully outside and largest halfway through the band. They are recombined per point, so a warp only costs steps where it is actually doing something — an untouched corner of the scene marches at full speed.

What follows the warp, and what does not

Because warping happens inside the two functions everything else already funnels through, the whole renderer inherits it consistently and for free: normals, ambient occlusion, shadows, reflections and refraction, the 2D slice, click-to-select and its outline, the empty-space accelerator's grid, and the photon map's paths all see the deformed shape. Materials follow too — a colour strip stays stuck to the structure it belongs to rather than sliding off it when the warp moves.

What is not warped is anything that is not the fractal. Rays stay straight; fog emitters and lights sit in plain world space. Bending a ray's path, and portals and mirrors, are designed but not built — the editor offers only the deformations that do something, which is why there is no "kind" choice beyond the seven above.

Follow spatial warpdefault offcamera group

A checkbox in the Fractals panel's Camera group, next to 2D mode. Off, the camera flies through undeformed world space and the warped fractal slides past it. On, the camera is carried through the fractal's own coordinates: it rolls where space twists, and its speed changes to cover the fractal at a constant rate rather than the world — slowing where space is stretched out and speeding up where it is crushed. Flying along a twist's axis therefore spirals, which is the whole point.

The speed correction is clamped to a factor of ten either way. Space can compress arbitrarily hard near an inversion's clamp, and a camera that inherited that literally would either freeze or teleport. It is a no-op with no warps in the scene (the panel says so) and in 2D mode.

Animating a warp

Every number a warp's sliders can move is keyframed — centre, orientation, region size, falloff, strength, and all of the per-deformation parameters, including those of deformations this warp is not currently using, so switching a warp from twist to bend does not silently drop its animation. What is not animated is which deformation it is and which region shape it has: a keyframe interpolates values, and there is no halfway point between a twist and a sphere inversion. See Chapter 18.

Chapter 15

Depth of field & MC Render

Two optional camera and lighting effects, both in the Fractals panel, both applying to the preview and to renders alike.

Depth of field

A thin-lens simulation: instead of every ray starting from a single pinhole, several rays start from random points on a disc-shaped lens and are re-aimed at the same point on the focus plane. The preview takes 8 lens samples, an export takes 32 — so DOF reads as a soft grain live and a clean blur when rendered. With MC Render on, DOF converges along with everything else as frames accumulate.

Both effects need rays to work on, so neither does anything in 2D mode (Chapter 16). The panel says as much next to the depth-of-field sliders rather than hiding them, so the settings are visibly still there when you switch back.

Depth of fielddefault off

Enables the effect. With aperture at 0 it does nothing regardless.

Focus distancedefault 3.2range 0.1–20

How far from the camera the sharp zone is centred, measured along each pixel's own view ray so the focus surface stays perpendicular to the view rather than flattening at the frame edges.

Focus range (sharp zone width)default 0.5range 0–5

How wide the fully sharp zone is, centred on the focus distance, before blur ramps in. A literal thin lens has a razor-thin focal plane, which on fractal geometry produces a thin sharp ring in a cloud of mush; this widens it into a usable band. At 0 you get the literal thin-lens behaviour.

Aperture (blur amount)default 0.05range 0–0.5

Lens radius. 0 is a pinhole with no blur; larger values blur harder outside the sharp zone and also widen the transition band into it.

MC Render

With MC Render off, ambient light is a flat constant scaled by occlusion. With it on, the renderer traces a real bounce ray from each visible surface and shades what it hits — genuine indirect light, picking up colour bleed from the fractal onto itself and from the sky into open areas.

One bounce sample per frame is far too noisy to look at, so the image is refined progressively: while the camera and scene sit still, successive frames are averaged together, and the panel shows a live Samples: n / target readout. Touching anything that changes the image restarts the average from zero.

MC Renderdefault off

Enables indirect light. While converging, the app keeps redrawing at frame rate rather than idling, so expect the GPU to stay busy for a few seconds after every change.

Live preview samples (quality target)value 256slider range 4–32

How many accumulated frames the preview aims for before it stops forcing extra redraws. The stored default is 256 but the slider's own maximum is 32, so the panel clamps it to 32 on sight — right-click the slider and raise Max if you want the preview to converge further.

Export samplesvalue 512slider range 16–128

The independent sample count used for still and animation renders, where there is no progressive display and every sample is paid up front. Same clamping caveat: it lands at 128 unless you widen the range. Render time scales directly with this number.

MC Render's export samples also pay for motion blur. If MC Render already asks for more accumulated draws than the motion blur sample count, the shutter is simply spread across the draws you were buying anyway and the blur costs nothing extra (Chapter 18).

Chapter 16

2D mode

2D mode, the first checkbox in the Fractals panel's Camera group, stops ray marching altogether and renders the flat cross section the camera cuts through the fractal. Instead of casting a ray per pixel, the renderer evaluates the fractal directly at the point that pixel sits on, and colours it from the same colour strips the 3D view uses.

Nothing about the scene changes when you tick it — the same fractals, the same offsets and rotations, the same strips. Only the camera's job changes: it stops being an eye and becomes a cutting plane.

What the plane is

The plane passes through the camera's position and is spanned by the camera's own right and up axes — it is the plane your screen was already showing you, made literal. Everything in front of and behind it is simply not in the picture.

That means every camera control keeps the meaning it had, and reads as something useful in 2D:

InputWhat it does to the slice

A / DSlide the window left / right across the slice
W / SPush the cutting plane forward / back through the fractal — this is how you move to a different cross section
Arrow keys, mouse dragRe-aim the plane, cutting at a different angle
Q / ERoll the plane, spinning the image
Z / CHalve / multiply the pan and slice-depth speed, as in 3D
Scroll wheelSteps the plane forward / back, same axis as W/S

Zoom is geometric: each moment you hold F multiplies the magnification rather than subtracting a fixed amount, so closing in feels the same however deep you already are. Pan and slice-depth speed scale with the zoom automatically, so A/D and W/S stay usable at every magnification instead of hurling you across the frame — you should need Z far less here than you do flying in 3D.

2D modedefault off

Switches the renderer from ray marching to slicing.

Zoom readoutF / R

Shows the current magnification and the slice window's half-height in world units. It is a readout, not a slider — the zoom spans too many orders of magnitude for one to be usable. Reset 2D zoom next to it returns to 1×.

Why there is no shading

A cross section has no surface facing the camera, so there is nothing for a light to fall on. Lights, shadows, ambient occlusion, reflections, refraction, subsurface, volumetric fog and depth of field are all simply absent in 2D mode — they are not disabled settings, there is no geometry for them to act on. Your lights and fog emitters are untouched and come straight back when you switch the mode off.

What you see instead is colour alone, and it is exact: a colour strip stop renders as precisely the colour you picked, with none of the tonemap compression a lit 3D surface goes through. This makes 2D mode a genuinely good way to author a strip — the gradient is easier to judge flat than wrapped around a lit surface.

Colour comes from the orbit trap, the same quantity the 3D view colours surfaces with, folded across instances by the same combine modes (Chapter 7). A scene therefore looks like itself in both modes.

The picture is drawn in two bands. Where the plane passes through solid fractal, the strip colour stands at full strength, faded out over the last pixel so the outline is antialiased rather than crawling as you zoom. Outside it, the distance estimate drives a dimmer falloff over a couple of dozen pixels, which keeps the surrounding structure faintly visible and shows you which way the shape continues — the classic distance-estimate look. That halo is measured in pixels, so it stays the same visual thickness at every magnification.

Zooming needs iterations

The outline is only as sharp as the formula's own Iterations parameter can resolve. Zoom far enough on a fixed iteration count and the boundary goes soft and eventually mushy, exactly as it does in 3D. Raise Iterations as you descend. The zoom itself stops at a slice half-height of 10−6, which is roughly where 32-bit floats give out and the image starts breaking into flat blocks.

Renders and stereo

2D mode is a property of the camera, so it flows through everything downstream unchanged: Export high-quality image…, tiled at any resolution, renders the same slice at the same zoom the viewport is showing (Chapter 17), and so does every frame of an animation render (Chapter 18). Export-only precision settings and fog samples have nothing to act on and cost nothing.

Render as stereoscopic works too, though it is worth knowing what it can and cannot mean. A flat picture has no depth to show off, so the pair fuses as one flat image floating at a single distance rather than as a solid you can look into. The two eyes slide the same plane sideways by the eye separation instead of toeing in on a convergence point the way they do in 3D — toe-in would tilt each eye's cutting plane a fraction of a degree and slice genuinely different structure into each half, which is the one thing stereo fusion cannot absorb. Convergence distance is therefore ignored in 2D; Eye separation sets how far in front of or behind the screen the flat image sits.

Animating a zoom

The 2D zoom is a keyframed value like any other camera parameter, so the obvious 2D animation — a long descent into one spot — is a two-keyframe job: zoom out at the start, zoom in at the end. It interpolates geometrically rather than linearly, so the magnification multiplies by an equal factor in every equal slice of time and the descent reads as steady. A linear interpolation across four decades would spend the first tenth of the clip covering ninety-nine percent of the distance and the rest looking frozen.

The mode itself is not animated, only the zoom — a keyframe cannot flip the scene between 2D and 3D partway through a render, the same way it cannot switch depth of field on and off.

MC Render is ignored in 2D mode, live and in renders alike. Progressive accumulation only buys something where a draw is random — a traced bounce, a lens sample, a dithered fog step — and the slice has none of those, so every extra sample would be a bit-identical copy of the last. It comes straight back when you switch the mode off. Motion blur in an animation is unaffected: it varies the scene between samples rather than re-rolling the same one.

Note

Gizmos still draw in 2D mode, projected straight down onto the cutting plane rather than in perspective — a selected fractal's triad reads as the shadow its offset casts onto the slice, which is what you want for lining an instance up with the structure in view.

Under the hood

2D mode is its own shader entry point with its own pipeline, not a branch inside the ray marcher's. That is a speed decision — sharing an entry point charged the slice the marcher's register footprint and crippled its occupancy — and it costs one extra, cheap pipeline compile per formula change. See Chapter 20.

Chapter 19

Scene files

Save scene… writes a .dream file — plain, indented JSON you can read and edit in a text editor. It stores the camera, every fractal with its transform, combine mode, mixins and color strip, every light, fog emitter and warp, the sky, both quality sets, export resolution, stereo and MC settings, every slider's custom min/max, and the whole keyframe timeline.

Load scene… replaces the entire live scene. Formula files are re-read from disk, each formula's saved parameter values are matched back onto it by name, and the shader is rebuilt once in the background — the status line reads "Loaded scene, compiling shader…" until that finishes.

Scenes reference formulas by path

A .dream file stores each formula's file path, not its contents. Moving, renaming, or deleting a .wgsl file breaks every scene that used it — that instance loads with a compile error in its editor while the rest of the scene loads normally. Editing a formula file, on the other hand, changes every scene that references it the next time they are loaded.

Matching parameters by name has a useful consequence: adding a new @param to a formula does not invalidate existing scenes. The new parameter takes the file's default; the ones that still exist keep their saved values. Renaming a parameter, however, resets it.

An environment image is stored the same way, as a path rather than as pixels, and decoded off disk when the scene opens (Chapter 12). A path that no longer resolves — a scene authored on another machine, or an HDRI since moved — reports the failure in the Sky panel's status line and renders the procedural gradient instead, rather than refusing to open the scene.

Files carry a version number. Missing fields take their defaults; a file written by a newer build is refused rather than loaded partially.

Chapter 20

Inside template.wgsl

src/gpu/shaders/template.wgsl is the renderer itself: the camera, the marcher, the lighting, the fog, the materials. It is a template, not a shader — it is never compiled as written. Everything specific to your scene is spliced into it at runtime, and the result is what the GPU actually receives.

How the shader is assembled

The template carries four kinds of marker. There are always exactly four @@FORMULA_N@@ and four @@MIXINS_N@@ markers, matching the four fractal slots, plus one @@DISPATCHERS@@ and one @@WARPS@@. Assembly does five things:

  1. Splices each formula's source into its numbered marker. Slot i is fractal i's own formula; slots 4 + i×3 + j are its three mixins. Sixteen slots exist in every compiled shader whether you use them or not.
  2. Renames every top-level function in each spliced file. The three contract functions get a _slot suffix; any other helper the file declares gets _fslot. This is what lets two formulas that both define a helper called fold, or the same file loaded into two slots at once, coexist without colliding.
  3. Wraps each base formula in a generated formula_N function implementing the ordinary non-mixed behaviour: iterate de_iterations times, track the orbit trap, finalize.
  4. Generates the dispatchers at @@DISPATCHERS@@ — eval_step and eval_finalize, static sixteen-armed switches over the slot number. WGSL has no function pointers, so slot dispatch has to be a switch, and it has to be generated because the arm count follows the instance and mixin limits.
  5. Chooses the warp evaluators at @@WARPS@@ — the real warp_domain and warp_step_limit when the scene has a warp in it, and an identity pair of stubs when it does not, so every scene_de, hit_material and march call site folds back to exactly what it compiled to before warps existed. That is worth about five seconds of cold compile, which is why it is a splice rather than template text (Chapter 14).

Slots you are not using are filled with a deliberately trivial filler formula — zero iterations, a huge constant distance — rather than a real one. Since dispatch happens on a slot number only known at runtime, the compiler cannot prove an unused arm is dead and compiles it anyway.

Consequence for you

WGSL is only validated at runtime, when the GPU driver compiles it. zig build will happily build an app whose shader cannot compile. Any change to a .wgsl file needs the app actually launched to know whether it works.

The formula contract

Every formula file — an instance's own or a mixin's — must define exactly these three functions:

fn de_iterations(p: array<f32, 8>) -> i32
fn de_step(carry: IterCarry, pos: vec3f, p: array<f32, 8>) -> IterCarry
fn de_finalize(carry: IterCarry) -> f32

The contract, checked before every compile

They share one small state struct:

struct IterCarry {
    z: vec3f,   // the iterated point
    dr: f32,    // running derivative estimate
}

de_step applies one iteration of the formula to that state and returns the result. de_iterations reports how many times it wants to be called when it is running standalone. de_finalize turns the final state into a distance estimate. p holds up to eight tunable parameters, filled from the sliders.

IterCarry holds only these two fields on purpose. In a mixin cycle, the formula whose de_step happened to run last is not necessarily the one that finalizes, so any formula's de_finalize must be computable from whatever state is sitting there. The built-in Mandelbulb demonstrates the discipline: it recomputes length(carry.z) fresh rather than trusting a carried "last radius" that some other formula's step may have clobbered.

Parameters

Sliders come from magic comments placed above de_iterations, in the order you want them exposed:

// @param Power min=2 max=16 default=8
// @param Iterations min=2 max=16 default=6 int

The first declared becomes p[0], the second p[1], and so on to p[7]. Anything undeclared is 0. The parser reads the whole file line by line looking for @param, so the comments technically may sit anywhere — but keeping them above de_iterations keeps the indices readable.

The trailing int is optional and marks a parameter the formula reads as a whole number — an iteration count it passes through i32(…), typically. Its slider then steps whole units and rounds anything typed into it, instead of moving continuously like every other slider. Every bundled formula marks its Iterations that way. It is a property of the formula source, not of the scene: it is re-read on every compile and never saved to a .dream file.

What the shader does with a pixel

fs_main
One fragment per pixel, for the ray-marched view. Builds the camera ray, then decides how many lens samples this pixel needs — one, or 8/32 if depth of field is on and this pixel sits outside the sharp zone.
trace_path
One loop, walked once per path vertex. Each turn marches to the next interface — through open space or through solid material, depending on which side of a surface the ray is currently on — integrates the volume along that segment, shades the share that stays, and continues along the share that leaves.
march
Sphere tracing: step along the ray by whatever distance the scene reports as safe, until the distance drops below the hit epsilon or the step budget runs out. In high-quality mode each step is verified by bisection first.
scene_de
Distance to the whole scene: applies every warp to the sample point once (warp_domain), evaluates each fractal instance in turn in that deformed domain, and folds them together with that instance's combine mode. Warping here and in hit_material is what makes every consumer — normals, AO, shadows, the slice, the accelerator — inherit it without knowing about it.
instance_de_trap
Undoes the instance's offset, rotation and scale to get into its local space, evaluates its formula (or its mixin cycle), then scales the resulting distance back and applies the step-safety factor.
eval_formula / eval_mixed
Either the generated single-formula wrapper, or the cycling fusion that interleaves base and mixin steps and finalizes with the base. Both also return the orbit trap value used for coloring.
estimate_normal, hit_material, calc_ao
At a hit: a four-tap tetrahedral gradient for the normal, the color strip sampled by orbit trap and blended across combine seams, and ambient occlusion probed along the normal.
shade_local
The share of the light that stays at a surface: per-light diffuse and specular with marched shadows, plus whatever the photon map deposited nearby. Where a stop’s Subsurface scattering is above 0, one extra march into the interior measures the material’s depth and adds an unshadowed back-scatter term. With MC Render on, the primary hit’s ambient term is a traced indirect bounce instead of a constant — reduced, while the photon map is on, to a sky-visibility probe, since the map already carries everything else.
scatter_at
What the surface does to the ray, in one place: Fresnel, Snell, total internal reflection, roughness and dispersion, chosen from Reflectiveness, Transparency, IOR and Roughness. Reports the diffuse share back for shade_local to handle deterministically and picks one of reflect or transmit to continue along, weighted so the expected value is the full split. The photon trace calls the same function with the diffuse branch sampled instead, which is what makes a camera ray and a photon meet glass identically.
march_fog
Front-to-back volumetric integration along the current path segment, accumulating in-scattered light and transmittance, with a per-light visibility march at each step plus a lookup of the photons that passed nearby. Runs on every segment, not just the first — which is what lets you see a beam converge inside a glass object, and what finally puts fog behind a transparent one on screen.
blit.wgsl
Tone-mapping happens here, not in the fragment shader — so MC Render can accumulate many frames of linear HDR light before the nonlinear compression, rather than averaging already-compressed colors. 2D mode pre-cancels this curve (undo_tonemap) so an unlit strip colour survives it exactly, instead of a pure white stop arriving as mid grey.

2D mode does not appear anywhere in that chain. It is a second @fragment entry point, fs_slice, driven by its own pipeline built from the same module — three lines of plane arithmetic and one call to slice_color, which evaluates hit_material at the plane point and masks its colour with the distance estimate. hit_material returns the folded distance alongside the material precisely so this path needs no scene_de call site of its own; see the compile-time note below.

Why the code looks the way it does

Three structural oddities in the file are load-bearing, and worth understanding before editing it.

Every duplicated call site multiplies the whole shader. GPU compilers do not emit real function calls in a fragment shader — they inline the entire call graph into one flat entry point and then optimise that. scene_de expands to every one of the sixteen compiled formula slots, and it is itself inlined at around thirty places. So writing the four normal-estimation taps as four straight-line calls instead of a loop means four more copies of every formula, thirty times over. The loops in estimate_normal, calc_ao, visibility_march, and the single trace_path call in fs_main all exist for this reason.

2D mode is a separate entry point for occupancy, not for tidiness. A GPU allocates a shader's registers for its worst path, not the one a given draw takes, and how many waves can run concurrently per SIMD falls out of that allocation. As a branch inside fs_main the slice would inherit the register footprint of the whole march-and-shade tree and run at a fraction of its occupancy. Compiled alone it needs a fraction of those registers, and it expands the formula tree once against fs_main's thirty-odd.

Cold compiles are the normal case. Any formula edit changes the generated code and therefore misses the driver's on-disk shader cache. A warm compile is near-instant and tells you nothing about the cold one.

Writing a formula

A formula file is a small standalone .wgsl file. It can declare its own helper functions freely — they get uniquified per slot — but module-scope constants are not renamed, so keep constants inside the functions that use them, and do not declare anything the template already provides (IterCarry, the uniform block, the smooth-min family, and so on are all already in scope). Chapter 22 walks through building a formula from nothing and lists every helper you can call.

A minimal, complete example

This is the built-in Mandelbulb, which is also a good starting skeleton:

// @param Power min=2 max=16 default=8
// @param Iterations min=2 max=16 default=6
fn de_iterations(p: array<f32, 8>) -> i32 {
    return i32(p[1]);
}

fn de_step(carry: IterCarry, pos: vec3f, p: array<f32, 8>) -> IterCarry {
    let power = p[0];
    let r = length(carry.z);
    if (r > 2.0) {
        return carry;              // escaped: further calls are no-ops
    }
    let theta = acos(clamp(carry.z.z / r, -1.0, 1.0)) * power;
    let phi   = atan2(carry.z.y, carry.z.x) * power;
    let zr    = pow(r, power);
    let dr    = pow(r, power - 1.0) * power * carry.dr + 1.0;
    let z     = zr * vec3f(sin(theta) * cos(phi),
                           sin(theta) * sin(phi),
                           cos(theta)) + pos;
    return IterCarry(z, dr);
}

fn de_finalize(carry: IterCarry) -> f32 {
    let r = length(carry.z);
    let log_est = 0.5 * log(max(r, 1e-6)) * r / max(carry.dr, 1e-6);
    return min(log_est, r);        // capped: see below
}

src/gpu/shaders/mandelbrot.wgsl

Rules that matter

  • Make de_step idempotent after escape. A mixin cycle can call your step more times than de_iterations ever asks for. Once your formula considers itself escaped, return carry unchanged so the extra calls are harmless.
  • Do not trust carried state you did not write. In a mix, the previous step may have come from another formula. Recompute what you need from z and dr rather than assuming anything else survived.
  • Fold from carry.z, not from pos. pos is the original sample point (the "c" of an escape-time formula); carry.z is whatever the previous iteration — possibly another formula's — produced. A formula that recomputes each level from pos cannot be mixed; the shipped Menger sponge is written as a genuine iterative fold specifically so that it can be.
  • Never return a distance larger than the true one. Overestimating lets the marcher step straight through your surface, which is what patchy or missing geometry looks like. Underestimating is always safe, just slower. The Mandelbulb's min(log_est, r) cap exists for exactly this: the log-escape estimate grows faster than the shape's own radius at large r, and would let the marcher tunnel through distant parts of the fractal.
  • Bounded folds need a different finalize. An escape-time formula diverges, so length(z)/dr is a real distance. A fold like the Menger sponge never diverges, so the same expression is small nearly everywhere and starves the marcher — those formulas should return the distance to a bounding shape instead, scaled down by dr.
  • Keep it cheap. Your file is compiled into up to sixteen slots and inlined at roughly thirty call sites. Trigonometry and branches multiply accordingly.

The workflow

  1. Write the file, ideally into formulas/ with the name_label_label.wgsl convention so it shows up in the library browser with filters.
  2. Point a fractal's formula slot at it and press Compile. Errors appear in red under the button; the previous formula stays loaded, so a broken edit never leaves you with a black screen.
  3. If the parameter sliders are wrong or missing, check the @param spelling — an unparseable declaration is skipped silently, and a formula with no valid declarations simply gets no sliders.
  4. Expect the first compile of a new formula to take a few seconds. Subsequent compiles of the same combination are cached by the driver.

Common error messages: "File must define `fn de_step(…)`" means one of the three contract functions is missing or spelled differently. "Could not read file" is a path problem. "A shader is already compiling" means you clicked Compile while another was in flight — wait and click again. Anything else in red is the GPU driver's own WGSL compiler message, quoted verbatim.

Chapter 22

Formula tutorial & function reference

Chapter 21 states the contract a formula file has to meet. This chapter is the other half: it builds three formulas from an empty file, one pattern at a time, and then lists every helper the template puts at a formula's disposal. Read the tutorial once; keep the reference.

What you are really writing

The renderer never asks "is this point inside the fractal?". It asks a cheaper question, millions of times a frame: "how far can I step from here without crossing the surface?". A formula is a function that answers it — a distance estimate. The marcher takes the number as a safe step, moves that far along the ray, asks again, and stops when the answer drops below the hit tolerance.

Every formula in NumericDream answers it the same way. de_step folds space, once per iteration: it moves the point somewhere else and keeps a running tally in dr of how much the folds so far have stretched distances. de_finalize measures a distance in the folded space — to the origin, to a unit cube, to whatever shape the fold keeps returning to — and divides by the tally to turn it back into a distance in the space the point started in. Almost every rule in this chapter falls out of that division: the tally has to be right, and the measurement has to be honest.

Where your formula runs

pos arrives in the instance's local space. The instance's Offset has already been subtracted, its Rotation undone and its per-axis Scale divided out, so a formula never needs position, rotation or size parameters of its own — the instance supplies them. The distance you return is in local units; the renderer scales it back up and applies the Step-size safety factor afterwards (instance_de_trap, Chapter 20).

When the instance has no mixins, this is the wrapper that calls you, generated once per slot:

var carry = IterCarry(pos, 1.0);          // z starts at the point, dr at 1
var trap = 1e6;
let iters = de_iterations(p);
for (var it = 0; it < iters; it++) {
    carry = de_step(carry, pos, p);
    trap = min(trap, length(carry.z));   // orbit trap, for the colour strip
}
return vec2f(de_finalize(carry), trap);

The generated formula_N wrapper

Two things to take from it. de_iterations is consulted only here — in a mixin cycle the three sliders in Chapter 9 decide how often your step runs, and it may be more often than you asked for. And the orbit trap is not yours to define: it is always the smallest length(z) seen across the iterations, divided by 1.5 and clamped to 0–1 to pick a position along the colour strip. A formula whose iterates linger near the origin colours from the left end of the strip; one whose iterates fly outward early colours from the right. You steer it only through what your fold does to z.

Step 1 — a shape with no iterations

Start with the smallest file that renders, and keep it: it is the pattern for every primitive. Save this as formulas/sphere_primitive_round.wgsl.

// Sphere: a unit sphere, scaled by Size.

// @param Size min=0.02 max=8 default=1
fn de_iterations(p: array<f32, 8>) -> i32 {
    return primitive_de_iterations();          // exactly one step
}

fn de_step(carry: IterCarry, pos: vec3f, p: array<f32, 8>) -> IterCarry {
    return primitive_de_step(carry, p[0]);     // shrink space so the shape is unit-sized
}

fn de_finalize(carry: IterCarry) -> f32 {
    return (length(carry.z) - 1.0) / max(abs(carry.dr), 1e-6);
}

Point a fractal's formula slot at it, press Compile, and tick Simple render → Geometry only so you are looking at nothing but shape. You should see a ball; drag Size and it grows.

Follow the numbers through. primitive_de_step divides z by Size and records 1 / Size in dr — space has been shrunk by that factor. The finalize measures the distance to a unit sphere in the shrunken space, then divides by dr, which multiplies by Size again: (|pos| / Size − 1) × Size = |pos| − Size, the exact distance to a sphere of radius Size. That round trip — measure in a convenient space, divide by the stretch — is the whole method.

Notice what the finalize is not given: it has no p. A parameter can reach the finished distance only by way of something the step put into z or dr, which is why Size travels through dr and why the bundled primitives have exactly one slider. A torus with an adjustable tube thickness would need the thickness inside de_finalize, and cannot get it there.

Things to try, changing only the finalize:

  • Torus: let q = vec2f(length(carry.z.xz) - 1.0, carry.z.y); return (length(q) - 0.35) / max(abs(carry.dr), 1e-6);
  • Octahedron: return (abs(carry.z.x) + abs(carry.z.y) + abs(carry.z.z) - 1.0) * 0.57735 / max(abs(carry.dr), 1e-6);. The 0.57735 (1/√3) turns a plane equation into a true distance; without it the estimate is up to √3 too large facing each face, and the marcher tunnels.
  • Open cube_primitive_polyhedron.wgsl for the inside-and-outside form that stays exact below the surface — which is what refraction and subsurface scattering march through.

Step 2 — a fold

Iterated function systems are the easiest real fractals to write, because the tally is trivial: every step multiplies space by one number. This is the Menger sponge's fold with its scale exposed and a twist added. Save it as formulas/twistcube_ifs_cubic_twist.wgsl.

// Twist cube: the Menger fold with adjustable scale and a per-iteration twist.

// @param Scale min=1.2 max=4 default=3
// @param TwistDeg min=-90 max=90 default=0
// @param Iterations min=0 max=12 default=5 int
fn de_iterations(p: array<f32, 8>) -> i32 {
    return i32(p[2]);
}

fn de_step(carry: IterCarry, pos: vec3f, p: array<f32, 8>) -> IterCarry {
    let scale = p[0];
    let twist = radians(p[1]);

    var z = rot_y(twist) * carry.z;               // 1. rotate: no stretch, dr untouched
    z = sort_desc_abs(z);                         // 2. fold into one wedge of one octant
    z = z * scale - vec3f(scale - 1.0);           // 3. blow up around the corner (1,1,1)
    if (z.z < -0.5 * (scale - 1.0)) {
        z.z += scale - 1.0;                       // 4. the tunnel through the middle
    }
    return IterCarry(z, carry.dr * scale);        // 5. record the stretch
}

fn de_finalize(carry: IterCarry) -> f32 {
    let q = abs(carry.z) - vec3f(1.0);            // distance to the unit cube…
    return length(max(q, vec3f(0.0))) / carry.dr; // …in folded space, unstretched
}

Compile it and set Iterations to 0. You get a cube: with no steps, z is still pos, dr is 1, and the finalize measures a plain unit cube. Now raise Iterations one notch at a time and watch the sponge appear. Each step takes the cube, folds all eight octants onto one, and blows that octant up by Scale around its outer corner — so the unit cube in folded space stands for a cube one Scale-th the size in the space before it. A point is in the sponge if its folded image is still inside the cube after every step.

Line by line:

  1. A rotation moves points without changing the distances between them, so dr is left alone. At 0° it is the identity; at 20° the sponge's layers twist as they shrink.
  2. sort_desc_abs mirrors the point into the positive octant and then sorts the axes so x ≥ y ≥ z — a further fold into one sixth of the octant. Mirrors and axis swaps also preserve distances.
  3. Multiplying by Scale stretches every distance by Scale. Subtracting scale − 1 is a translation chosen so the corner (1,1,1) stays put.
  4. The Menger tunnel is a conditional translation — also free.
  5. Only line 3 stretched anything, so dr grows by exactly Scale. The finalize divides by it, and the estimate is honest. (If Scale could go negative you would multiply by abs(scale).)

The finalize's cube has to be the fold's own stable shape. Line 3 has its fixed point at 1 on every axis, so the set the fold keeps returning to lives inside the unit cube; measure to anything smaller and holes open up, to anything larger and the sponge grows flesh. That is why the corner is not a slider: an adjustable corner would need the finalize to know the new cube, and it cannot. Change the overall size with the instance's Scale instead.

Things to try:

  • Swap sort_desc_abs for tetra_fold and delete the tunnel: a Sierpinski tetrahedron. Its stable set is not a cube, so switch the finalize to de_finalize_plain(carry).
  • Swap rot_y for rot_z, or apply two rotations in a row. Every one of them is free.
  • Scale 2 gives a solid cube (nothing ever leaves the fold); 2.5 gives thick bars; 4 gives thin ones. Beyond about 5 the bars are thinner than the hit tolerance can resolve.

Step 3 — an escape-time formula

Escape-time formulas add the original point back in every iteration — z ← f(z) + pos — so where the sequence goes depends on where it started. The tally has to account for that, and the finalize changes, because the sequence diverges instead of settling into a shape. This is the Mandelbox fold with a switchable Julia constant; save it as formulas/switchbox_box_boxfold_julia.wgsl.

// Switch box: the Mandelbox fold, iterated on the point or on a fixed Julia constant.

// @param Scale min=-3 max=3 default=2
// @param MinRadius min=0.05 max=1 default=0.5
// @param FixedRadius min=0.5 max=3 default=1
// @param Iterations min=1 max=30 default=12 int
// @param Julia min=0 max=1 default=0 int
// @param JuliaX min=-2 max=2 default=0.4
// @param JuliaY min=-2 max=2 default=-0.6
// @param JuliaZ min=-2 max=2 default=0.1
fn de_iterations(p: array<f32, 8>) -> i32 {
    return i32(p[3]);
}

fn de_step(carry: IterCarry, pos: vec3f, p: array<f32, 8>) -> IterCarry {
    let scale = p[0];
    let min_radius2 = p[1] * p[1];
    let fixed_radius2 = p[2] * p[2];
    let c = select(pos, vec3f(p[5], p[6], p[7]), p[4] > 0.5);

    var z = box_fold(carry.z, 1.0);                                // reflect the outer shell inward
    let sf = sphere_fold(z, carry.dr, min_radius2, fixed_radius2); // invert the inner ball
    z = sf.xyz * scale + c;
    let dr = sf.w * abs(scale) + 1.0;
    return IterCarry(z, dr);
}

fn de_finalize(carry: IterCarry) -> f32 {
    return de_finalize_scaled(carry);
}

Three new things are happening.

The sphere fold changes the tally itself. Inverting in a sphere is not a uniform scale — it stretches points near the centre far more than points near the rim — so sphere_fold takes dr in and hands it back in the fourth component of its result, multiplied by whatever factor it applied. Always take dr from the helper; never assume you know what it did.

The + 1.0. The scale multiplies the tally as in Step 2, and then one is added. That one is the derivative of the + c term with respect to the starting point: because pos is added fresh each iteration, the sequence's sensitivity to it is one more than what the earlier iterations passed along. Leave it out and the tally is too small, the divided distance too large, and the shape develops holes. For a Julia variant (Julia = 1) the constant does not depend on the starting point and the + 1 is not strictly needed — but keeping it only makes the estimate slightly more conservative, and every bundled Julia formula keeps it.

The finalize is length(z) / |dr|. A diverging sequence has no stable shape to measure to. Instead, how far out z has flown, over how much the map stretched to fling it there, is itself a distance estimate: the further outside the set a point starts, the sooner and faster it escapes. de_finalize_scaled uses abs(dr) so a negative Scale works — negative Mandelbox scales are a different, equally valid family of shapes.

The last parameter is the eighth. The file declares all eight, which is the limit: to add a FoldingLimit slider you would have to take one away. Julia is an integer used as a switch; select(a, b, cond) returns b when the condition holds, and both arms are always evaluated, so the switch costs nothing to keep in.

Bail-out

This file has no escape check, and does not need one: the box and sphere folds keep z bounded until the multiplication, so after thirty iterations at Scale 3 it is at most around 330 — large, but far inside 32-bit float range. A power map is different. The Mandelbulb's pow(r, 8) on a point already at radius 2 reaches 256, then 1019, then infinity, then NaN — and NaN poisons every comparison downstream, so the pixel goes black. That is why bulb_power_step returns carry unchanged once r > 2, and why Chapter 21 asks for that check to be idempotent. Put one in whenever a step can grow z faster than linearly.

Things to try:

  • Twist it: insert z = rot_z(radians(15.0)) * z; after the sphere fold. Free, as always.
  • Wedge-fold it: insert z = sort_desc_abs(z); before the box fold. Also free — and now it is a different fractal.
  • Replace both folds with bulb_power_step(carry, c, 8.0) and the finalize with bulb_log_escape(carry): a Mandelbulb / Juliabulb switch.

Step 4 — your own helpers

Anything you find yourself writing twice belongs in a helper function above the contract functions. The assembler renames every function your file declares with a per-slot suffix, so two files can both define a fold, and one file can sit in all sixteen slots at once, without a clash. Here is a polar wedge fold — the one prismkifs_box_boxfold_wedgefold.wgsl writes inline — as a helper:

// Fold the XY plane into one wedge of angle 2π/n, mirrored about the wedge's centre line.
fn wedge_fold(z: vec3f, n: f32) -> vec3f {
    const TAU = 6.28318530717959;               // function-scope const: private to this function
    let period = TAU / n;
    var a = wrap_angle(atan2(z.y, z.x), period);
    if (a > period * 0.5) {
        a = period - a;
    }
    let r = length(z.xy);
    return vec3f(r * cos(a), r * sin(a), z.z);
}

Drop it into Step 2's file and call it on the first line of the step — var z = wedge_fold(carry.z, 5.0); — for five-fold symmetry. A wedge fold is rotations and one mirror, so dr is untouched.

Three renaming traps

Only functions are renamed. A module-scope const, var or struct keeps its name, so the moment the file is loaded into a second slot — or another file happens to declare the same name — the shader fails with a redeclaration error. Keep constants inside the function that uses them, as above, or write the literal.

The renamer reads comments. It finds function names by scanning the whole file for the word fn followed by an identifier. A comment such as // like fn sphere_fold but… registers sphere_fold as one of your functions and renames every call to it, which then points at a function that does not exist. Write "like sphere_fold" instead.

Shadowing works, on purpose. If you declare your own box_fold, every call in your file is renamed along with it and reaches yours, not the template's. Handy for trying a variant of a helper — just do not name a helper after a WGSL built-in such as clamp or mix, for the same reason.

Making it mixable

Nothing extra is required for a formula to work as a mixin, but three habits make it work well, and all three are about the fact that the carry you receive may have been written by a different formula (Chapter 9):

  • Fold carry.z, never pos. pos is the starting point and may be added, but a step that rebuilds its state from pos ignores everything the other formulas in the cycle did.
  • Recompute anything you need from z and dr at the top of the step. There is nowhere else to keep state, and that is deliberate.
  • Make your finalize sensible on a carry you did not produce. length(z) / dr and the unit-cube distance both are; a finalize that assumes z lies on some particular manifold is not.

Remember that in a mix your de_iterations is ignored and your step may run more often than it would alone, so any escape check must keep returning carry unchanged once it fires.

Testing and debugging

The app is the only validator: WGSL is compiled by the GPU driver at Compile time, and a syntax error appears in red under the button with the driver's own message. Once it compiles, the shape is the diagnostic.

  • Geometry only (Chapter 4) is where to iterate. It runs from a much smaller shader, so compiles and frames are both fast, and nothing but the distance estimate is on screen.
  • 2D mode (Chapter 16) shows your distance field, not just its surface. The glow around the cross-section is drawn from the estimate itself: with an honest estimate it is an even band about 24 pixels wide hugging the outline. Where the band fattens, your estimate is too small there (slow, but safe); where it thins away before the outline, your estimate is too large, and the marcher will tunnel through that face in 3D.
  • Step-size safety factor is the knob that tells you whether a problem is yours. If speckle clears at 0.5, the formula over-estimates somewhere; if it does not, something else is wrong.

Reference: the contract

struct IterCarry {
    z: vec3f,   // the folded point
    dr: f32,    // accumulated stretch factor; starts at 1.0
}

fn de_iterations(p: array<f32, 8>) -> i32
fn de_step(carry: IterCarry, pos: vec3f, p: array<f32, 8>) -> IterCarry
fn de_finalize(carry: IterCarry) -> f32

All three must be present, spelled exactly, and take exactly these parameters. IterCarry(z, dr) constructs one. p is the eight slider values in declaration order; undeclared entries are 0. Anything else the file declares is yours.

@param grammar

// @param Name min=<number> max=<number> default=<number> [int]
  • Name is one token — no spaces — and is cut at 23 characters. It is the slider's label.
  • min, max and default may come in any order and may be omitted; the fallbacks are 0, 1 and 0. A key whose value does not parse is ignored and the others are still read.
  • int makes the slider step in whole units and round typed values. It changes nothing in the shader — you still read the value as f32 and truncate it yourself with i32(…).
  • The line is recognised anywhere @param appears, but only the first eight declarations count.

Reference: folding helpers

Everything below is defined by the template and in scope for every formula. Where a helper changes the tally, its entry says so; "dr unchanged" means you pass your own dr straight through.

box_fold(z: vec3f, limit: f32) -> vec3fdr unchanged

Reflects each component that lies beyond ±limit back inward across that plane: clamp(z, −limit, limit) × 2 − z. The Mandelbox's outer fold.

sphere_fold(z: vec3f, dr: f32, min_radius2: f32, fixed_radius2: f32) -> vec4fdr in .w

Sphere inversion with a floor. Points inside the minimum radius are scaled by a constant fixed_radius2 / min_radius2; points between the two radii are inverted, scaled by fixed_radius2 / r²; points outside pass through. Plain inversion in a sphere of squared radius radius2 about center: points inside are pushed out, points outside pass through. The Kleinian's fold. Same return convention as sphere_fold.

Wraps the point into the cell of size cell centred on the origin, so the rest of the step repeats across an infinite lattice. A translation.

abs(z) followed by a sort so that x ≥ y ≥ z: folds all of space into one 48th of it. The cubic-symmetry fold behind the Menger sponge.

Three conditional mirrors across the planes x + y = 0, x + z = 0, y + z = 0: the tetrahedral-symmetry fold behind the Sierpinski tetrahedron.

Not a helper, but the pattern the icosahedral formulas use for an arbitrary mirror plane through the origin with unit normal n: let t = dot(z, n); if (t > 0.0) { z -= 2.0 * t * n; }. Any symmetry group can be built from a handful of these.

Syntax in one screen

  • let x = …; is immutable, var x = …; is mutable, const is a compile-time constant. Types are inferred; let z: vec3f = … if you want them stated.
  • No implicit conversion between integers and floats. p[1] is an f32; a loop count is i32(p[1]). Write float literals as 1.0; a bare 1 adapts to context, but 1 / 2 is integer division.
  • vec3f(1.0) splats; vec3f(a, b, c) constructs; z.xz swizzles; z.x = … assigns one component of a var.
  • if (…) { } else if (…) { } else { } — braces are always required. for (var i = 0; i < n; i++) { }. while (…) { }. break, continue, return.
  • No recursion, no function pointers, no default arguments, no overloading of your own functions. Every function must be declared at module scope.
  • Comments are // and /* … */.

What is in scope but off limits

Because your file is spliced into the whole renderer, every function and variable in template.wgsl is visible to it. Almost none of them belong in a formula:

  • scene_de, march, instance_de_trap, eval_formula, eval_step, hit_material, the photon and fog functions. These call you. WGSL forbids recursion, so calling any of them from a formula makes a cycle and the compile fails.
  • The uniform block u. Readable, but everything a formula legitimately needs from it already arrives as pos and p. u.time in particular is the viewport's clock and is 0 for every exported still and animation frame, so nothing built on it can be rendered — animate parameters through the Timeline instead (Chapter 18).
  • Textures and the photon storage buffers. Sampling inside a distance function is not meaningful, and a formula runs in every pass — the accelerator's and the photon tracer's compute passes included — so anything it touched would have to exist in all of them.
  • Anything named in the fixed-limits table of Chapter 20. The constants are readable, but a formula has no reason to.

The list of what a formula should reach for is the reference above, plus the built-ins. Everything else is the renderer's business.

Chapter 23

Troubleshooting

Surfaces look speckled, torn, or dotted

An overshooting distance estimate. Lower the fractal's Step-size safety factor first — this is what it is for, and it is especially likely on mixed shapes. If that alone does not do it, raise Bisection refine steps and lower the hit epsilon values.

Parts of a distant fractal simply do not render

Either the ray gave up early — raise Max march steps or Max distance — or a custom formula's de_finalize is overestimating at large radius, letting the first step tunnel straight through. See the cap rule in Chapter 21.

Detail looks melted or blobby up close

Lower Hit epsilon floor. The normal-estimation radius follows the hit tolerance, so when the tolerance is coarser than the actual feature size, the taps straddle different walls and the normal comes out as their average.

Fog shows concentric banding

Raise Fog samples in Render settings. Fog is dithered to trade banding for grain; more passes average the grain out. In an animation leave it at 1 — neighbouring frames do that averaging for free.

The image is grainy with MC Render on

It has not converged. Watch the Samples: n / target readout and let it settle; any change to the scene restarts it. For renders, raise Export samples — remembering that its slider maximum clamps the default down, so you likely need to widen the range first.

Everything freezes for many seconds after a formula change

That is a cold shader compile, and it is expected. The app deliberately stops rendering while compiling because it is not safe to talk to the GPU from two places at once. If it never finishes and the app exits reporting a lost device, the compile took long enough for the driver to decide the GPU had hung — simplify the formula, or reduce the number of active fractals and mixins, since every slot is compiled whether it is used or not.

Renders are extremely slow

In rough order of impact: fog samples × fog emitters, MC render samples, shadow-casting lights, resolution, stereo (doubles everything), motion blur samples, march steps. Turning off Cast shadows on fill lights and dropping fog samples usually recovers the most time for the least visible loss.

The 2D slice is mostly black, or its outline has gone soft

You have zoomed past what the formula's Iterations can resolve — the distance estimate stops reaching the surface, so the cross section stops filling in. Raise Iterations. If raising it no longer helps and the image is breaking into flat blocks, you have hit the floor of 32-bit float precision at a slice half-height around 10−6; the zoom stops there.

Surfaces tear only where a warp is acting

The warp's stretch bound is not holding — lower that warp's Step safety. If the fractal was already marginal before the warp, lower the instance's Step-size safety factor too. The reverse case, a warped region that marches absurdly slowly while looking fine, is a pessimistic bound: raise Step safety instead.

An HDRI loads but the scene still looks flatly lit

Check the Sky panel's status line for the word HDR. A PNG or JPEG cannot store anything brighter than white, so its sun is no brighter than its clouds and it lights a scene evenly whatever you do to Sky brightness (Chapter 12). Use a .hdr file, or add a global light for the directional half.

The sun disc is visible but casts no shadows

It is part of the environment, not a light. Add a Global light pointing the same way for sharp shadows; the disc is what that light looks like when you face it.

Nothing happens when I press F or R

They only zoom in 2D mode (Chapter 16), and camera input is ignored entirely while the mouse is over a panel — move the pointer over the viewport first.

A slider will not go far enough

Right-click it and type a new Min or Max. The new range is remembered and saved with the scene.

A loaded scene shows a compile error on one fractal

Its formula file has moved or been deleted. Scenes store paths, not formula contents. Point the slot at the file's new location and press Compile; everything else in the scene loaded normally.

Text fields will not accept typing

Click into the field first — keyboard input goes to the camera unless a widget has focus and the pointer is over the interface.
